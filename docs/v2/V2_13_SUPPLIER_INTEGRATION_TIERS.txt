V2_13 — SUPPLIER INTEGRATION TIERS: FROM A PHONE TO AN ERP
===========================================================================

Written 2026-09-11. A PLAN, and a narrow one on purpose.

WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS FOR. Not to build tier 3 or tier 4 — there is no
supplier asking for either. It exists to answer one question:

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  WHAT WOULD WE REGRET NOT DOING TODAY, when a wholesaler with an ERP
  arrives eighteen months from now?
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Almost nothing, as it turns out. The tiers are additive and can be
built when they are needed. But THREE THINGS are cheap columns today
and migrations-plus-backfill later, and Part 3 is the real content of
this document. Everything else is context for why those three matter.

NOT TO BE CONFUSED WITH V2_08, despite the similar-sounding name. That
document is about third-party services WE CONSUME — airtime float,
delivery brokerage, WhatsApp. This one is about how SUPPLIERS CONNECT
TO US, which is the opposite direction and shares none of the same
concerns.


===========================================================================
PART 1 — THE FOUR TIERS
===========================================================================

1.1 THE PRINCIPLE, BORROWED FROM FOOD DELIVERY

  Uber Eats solved this problem before us and the shape is worth
  copying exactly: THE COST OF INTEGRATING MUST BE PROPORTIONAL TO THE
  VOLUME. Asking a one-person bakery to build an API client is absurd.
  Asking a national chain to retype orders off a tablet is equally
  absurd. So there is a ladder, and a supplier climbs it when their
  own volume justifies the effort.

    TABLET        no IT at all. The device IS the point of sale, the
                  dashboard, and the order screen.
    POS           orders injected into the till system they already
                  own (Toast, Clover, Lightspeed), so staff cannot
                  tell which channel an order came from.
    DIRECT API    their own middleware. Menus sync, orders inject
                  programmatically. Four to eight weeks and a contract.
                       — Uber Eats merchant docs; Toast; ChowNow; Deliverect

  THE DETAIL WORTH STEALING: at the POS tier, an order from Uber looks
  identical to a walk-in order. The integration's success condition is
  that the KITCHEN NEVER LEARNS A NEW HABIT. That is the standard a
  TruConnect integration should be held to as well — a supplier's
  warehouse staff should not have to know an order came from us.

1.2 A BORROWED WORD WE SHOULD NOT BORROW

  "1P vs 3P" gets used loosely to mean "managed vs self-serve". It does
  not mean that. On Amazon, 1P (Vendor Central) means AMAZON BUYS THE
  INVENTORY, owns it, and sets the retail price; 3P (Seller Central) is
  the marketplace where the seller keeps ownership and sets their own
  price.
                       — Pattern; Feedvisor; Amify; Sellermania

  TRUCONNECT IS 3P ONLY, AND SHOULD SAY SO. We never buy stock, never
  own inventory, never set a supplier's price. What we might borrow
  from Vendor Central is only that it is INVITE-ONLY AND HUMAN-
  ONBOARDED — a property of how somebody joins, not of who owns the
  goods. Using "1P" as shorthand for our managed tier would eventually
  mislead somebody into thinking we take inventory risk.

  So the vocabulary here is TIER 1-4, not 1P/3P.

1.3 THE TIERS AS THEY APPLY TO US

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  TIER 1 — APP-ONLY
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Who        a wholesaler with no systems. A phone, a notebook, maybe
             a spreadsheet nobody else can read.
  Operates   entirely in the mobile app. Adds products by hand, takes
             orders on the screen, marks them fulfilled.
  Needs      that the app be the whole operation, not a companion to
             something else.
  Status     BUILT.

  ON TABLETS, which is a product decision disguised as a sales one.
  Uber did not hand out tablets as a marketing giveaway; it was the
  cheapest way to onboard a merchant with no IT, and the hardware paid
  for itself in orders that would otherwise have been missed. For a
  town wholesaler whose digital orders are growing, a tablet is a
  materially better order screen than a phone — bigger, stays on a
  counter, stays charged, and is not also somebody's personal device
  that leaves the building at five o'clock.

  IT COSTS NOTHING ARCHITECTURALLY. The app is already React Native and
  Expo Router; a tablet is a large phone. What it changes is layout
  work (two-column at width) and, more importantly, the SALES motion:
  "we will set you up" is a different conversation from "download our
  app". Worth its own decision when supply acquisition becomes the
  bottleneck — not a build item today.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  TIER 2 — FILE
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Who        has a price list. Exports from Pastel/Sage/Excel, or
             keeps a spreadsheet a person maintains.
  Operates   uploads a file periodically; takes orders in the app.
  Needs      the importer to be forgiving of real files — merged cells,
             inconsistent units, missing barcodes.
  Status     BUILT. bulk_import, the matching ladder, the review queue.

  THIS IS THE TIER MOST SOUTH AFRICAN WHOLESALERS ACTUALLY OCCUPY, and
  it is where the catalogue work of V2_11 pays off. A supplier who can
  send a spreadsheet is not a supplier who can call an API, and the gap
  between those two is most of the market.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  TIER 3 — INBOUND API
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Who        has an ERP and somebody who can write against an API.
  Operates   their system pushes catalogue and prices to us on a
             schedule. Humans log in for REPORTS, not for order-taking.
  Needs      an API key, idempotent writes, and clear errors.
  Status     HALF-BUILT.

    Built      api_key_required, scoped keys, key authentication,
               the inbound product endpoints.
    Missing    a way to ISSUE a key to a supplier who never logs in.
               ApiKeyService.issue() exists; the only door to it is an
               HTTP route behind @role_required("supplier").
    Missing    idempotency. See Part 3.1 — this is the gap that
               matters.

  THE DASHBOARD QUESTION, ANSWERED. A tier 3 supplier still has an
  account and still logs in. Not to take orders — those land in their
  own system — but for reports and money, API key management, their
  terms and profile, disputes, and whether the integration is healthy.
  "Do they need a dashboard" and "do they need an order screen" are
  different questions, and only the second one gets a no.

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  TIER 4 — TWO-WAY
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Who        a partner whose warehouse runs on their own software.
  Operates   we push order events INTO their infrastructure; stock and
             price changes flow back to us.
  Needs      outbound webhooks with signing, retries and replay.
  Status     NOT BUILT AT ALL. Confirmed: the only webhook code in this
             codebase is INBOUND (Cloudinary scan results), and there
             is no outbound HTTP delivery anywhere in src/.

  Sketched in Part 6. It needs its own document before it needs code.

PART 3 — THE THREE SEAMS THAT MUST BE RIGHT NOW
===========================================================================

The test applied to each: IS THIS CHEAP TODAY AND EXPENSIVE LATER? All
three are columns and a habit now, versus a migration plus a backfill
of history that was never recorded.

3.1 IDEMPOTENCY ON WRITES

  THE FAILURE, CONCRETELY. A supplier's ERP posts a product. The
  connection times out at fifteen seconds. The ERP has no way to know
  whether we processed it, so — correctly — it retries. We create a
  SECOND product. Nobody notices until their catalogue has doubled.

  This cannot be engineered away, and that is the important part: if a
  request times out the client genuinely cannot tell whether the server
  processed it. Retrying is the right behaviour. The fix is on our side.
                       — Stripe, "Designing robust and predictable APIs
                         with idempotency"; brandur.org

  THE STANDARD SHAPE, which we should copy rather than invent:

    · the CLIENT generates the key (a v4 UUID), not the server
    · sent as a header on POST; meaningless on GET and DELETE, which
      are idempotent by definition
    · we store the resulting status code and body against that key,
      INCLUDING FAILURES — a retry of a request that 500ed returns the
      same 500, rather than re-running it
    · the stored parameters are compared: the same key with different
      parameters is an error, not a silent overwrite
    · keys expire (24 hours is Stripe's window)

  WHY NOW AND NOT AT TIER 3. Because the habit is what is expensive,
  not the table. If write endpoints are built for two years without an
  idempotency key, adding one later means revisiting every one of them
  and re-reasoning about what "the same request" means for each. Adding
  the column and the decorator now costs an afternoon and makes every
  subsequent endpoint inherit it.

  STATUS: NOT PRESENT. Several services are idempotent in their own
  semantics (revoking a key, adding a brand alias), which is a good
  habit and a different thing — there is no request-level idempotency
  key anywhere.

3.2 AN EVENT LOG FOR STOCK ORDERS

  THE FAILURE. A partner asks for order events pushed to their system.
  We build the delivery machinery — and discover there is nothing to
  deliver FROM. Webhooks replay from an event stream; without one, the
  first partner gets no history, and a delivery that fails at 02:00 has
  nothing to retry against.

  Retention of at least 30 days, an API to list events by type and time
  and delivery status, and manual replay for a single event or a range,
  are the baseline expectations of anyone consuming webhooks.
                       — Hookdeck; Codelit; AppMaster

  WHAT EXISTS AND WHAT DOES NOT. The pattern is already in this
  codebase and applied twice — LoanHistoryEvent and OrderHistoryEvent
  both record an append-only history. Both belong to the BUSINESS's own
  books. network/orders/models/ contains only stock_order and
  stock_order_payment: the marketplace transaction itself has no event
  record. Its state changes are updates to a row.

  SO THE SEAM IS A THIRD USE OF A PATTERN THIS CODEBASE ALREADY HAS,
  not a new idea. StockOrderEvent, written wherever the order's state
  changes: placed, accepted, declined, fulfilled, cancelled, paid.

  AND IT PAYS FOR ITSELF BEFORE TIER 4 ARRIVES. "Why did this order
  change?" is a support question that exists today and is currently
  answered by inference. An append-only log answers it directly, and
  the same rows are what a webhook eventually sends.

3.3 THE ORIGIN OF A WRITE

  THE FAILURE. A supplier edits a price in the app at 09:00. Their ERP
  pushes the old price at 09:05. Which is correct? Today there is no
  way to answer, because a price is a value with no memory of where it
  came from.

  At tier 3 and above, the ERP is USUALLY authoritative and the app is
  usually a convenience — but "usually" is a policy, and a policy needs
  a field to act on. Without one the only options are to let the last
  write win (silently reverting deliberate human edits) or to freeze
  app editing for integrated suppliers (a worse product).

  THE SEAM: a `source` on stock, price and product writes —
  APP / IMPORT / API — recorded at the moment of the write.

  CHEAP NOW, IMPOSSIBLE RETROSPECTIVELY. A column added later starts
  null for every existing row, and no amount of analysis recovers where
  a number came from two years ago.

  IT IS ALSO USEFUL IMMEDIATELY, which is the test of a good seam: "how
  many of this supplier's prices came from the importer versus typed by
  hand" is a question worth answering today.

  STATUS: NOT PRESENT.

PART 4 — IDENTITY, ORGANISATION, AND THE THING WE ARE NOT DOING
===========================================================================

4.1 THE CONFLATION

  Today one User row does three jobs:

    a PERSON who logs in
    the OWNER of a shopfront
    the IDENTITY an API key acts as

  These are the same thing right now. They stop being the same thing
  for the first supplier who has an ERP integration AND three staff who
  each need a login: that is one shopfront and four identities.

4.2 THE PROPER FIX, AND WHY NOT NOW

  The eventual answer is an ORGANISATION that owns the shopfront, with
  human users and machine identities both belonging to it. Service
  accounts — an identity with no interactive login, holding credentials
  — are how Google Cloud and AWS solve exactly this.

  IT IS ALSO A LARGE, INVASIVE REFACTOR touching auth, every ownership
  check, and every foreign key that points at a supplier. Doing it
  before a single supplier has asked for a second login would be the
  same mistake as building a permissions table for one admin, and for
  the same reason: a system with one subject cannot be tested against
  the case it exists for.

  THE TRIGGER IS SPECIFIC, so nobody has to guess: THE FIRST SUPPLIER
  WHO ASKS FOR A SECOND STAFF LOGIN. Not a date, not a headcount.

4.3 THE CHEAP HALF, WHICH IS WORTH DOING

  What makes the refactor expensive later is code that assumes
  supplier_id means A PERSON. Mostly it does not — it is used as "the
  shopfront owner", which survives the change untouched.

  So the discipline is narrow and costs nothing: DO NOT PUT
  PERSON-SPECIFIC DATA ON THE SUPPLIER PROFILE. A staff member's name,
  their notification preferences, their last login — those belong to a
  user, and putting them on the shopfront is what would make the
  eventual split painful.

PART 5 — WE ARE NEVER THEIR ONLY CHANNEL
===========================================================================

KFC has its own app and is also on Uber Eats. This is not indecision;
it is the normal state of every merchant. The own app keeps the whole
margin and the customer relationship. The marketplace supplies reach
they cannot build themselves. Same kitchen, same stock, two sources of
demand.

A WHOLESALER WITH A WEBSITE WILL DO EXACTLY THE SAME WITH US, and the
architectural consequence is a correctness problem rather than a
commercial one:

  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  If stock moves through a channel we cannot see, our numbers are
  wrong, and a business orders something that is not there.
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

TODAY WE IMPLICITLY ASSUME WE ARE AUTHORITATIVE. For tiers 1 and 2 that
is true enough — a supplier operating out of our app has no other
system to contradict it. It stops being true at tier 3, and that is the
real reason 3.3 (the origin of a write) matters more than it first
appears: it is the first step toward being able to say "their system is
the truth, ours is a cache".

THE HONEST POSITION FOR NOW: we are authoritative because nobody has
told us otherwise. Do not build sync machinery for a problem no
supplier has yet. Do record where numbers came from, so that when one
does, the conversation is a policy decision rather than an archaeology
project.

PART 6 — OUTBOUND WEBHOOKS (SKETCH ONLY)
===========================================================================

Deliberately a sketch. This needs its own document when a partner needs
it, and the research below is recorded so that document does not start
from nothing.

  AT-LEAST-ONCE IS THE SHAPE OF THE PROBLEM, not a flaw to engineer
  away. If a delivery times out we cannot know whether they processed
  it. So every event carries a stable id, and the consumer is told to
  key on it.

  THE OUTBOX PATTERN: the event is written in the SAME TRANSACTION as
  the state change it describes, and delivered by a separate worker.
  Anything else can commit an order and fail to record that it
  happened.

  SIGNING: HMAC-SHA256 over the exact request body plus a timestamp, in
  a header, with a per-endpoint secret. That is the industry standard
  and consumers already know how to verify it.

  RETRIES: five to eight attempts with exponential backoff over a 24-72
  hour window, then a dead-letter queue. Per-subscriber limits so one
  slow partner cannot starve the others.

  REPLAY: an API to list events by type, time and delivery status, and
  to replay one or a range. 30 days of retention minimum.
                       — Hookdeck; Codelit; AppMaster; Matheus Palma;
                         outbox pattern references

  AND THE PIECE THAT ALREADY EXISTS: src/shared/queue and the master
  scheduler give us the worker and the retry surface. What is missing
  is the outbox table (3.2) and the delivery machinery on top.
